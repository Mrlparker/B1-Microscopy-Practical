  3. 3 Focus on low power (4× objective): Use the coarse focus wheel to find the cells, then sharpen with fine focus.
  4. 4 Switch to high power (10× or 40×): Rotate to a higher-power lens and use only the fine focus wheel to sharpen one cell.

Unit Conversion Reminder: 1 millimetre (mm) = 1000 micrometres (μm) Eukaryotic plant and animal cells typically range from 10 μm to 100 μm in length (onion epidermal cells are extra-large storage cells at ~220 μm).

Q1. Why is iodine solution or methylene blue added to a slide before viewing under a light microscope? (2 marks)
Mark Scheme: Cells and sub-cellular structures are naturally transparent / colourless. The stain binds to specific structures (such as the nucleus or starch grains) to increase contrast so they become visible under the microscope.
Q2. Explain why you should always start focusing a slide using the lowest-power objective lens (4×). (2 marks)
Mark Scheme: The lowest-power lens provides the widest field of view, making it much easier to locate/find the specimen on the slide. It also has the largest working distance so you do not crack the slide or damage the objective lens when using the coarse focus wheel.
Q3. Describe how to place a coverslip onto a microscope slide and explain why this method is used. (2 marks)
Mark Scheme: Lower the coverslip slowly at an angle (45°) using a mounted needle. This pushes air out from underneath so air bubbles are not trapped, which would otherwise obscure the view of the cells.
Q4. A student views a human cheek cell at a total magnification of 400× and measures its image size as 24 mm. Calculate the actual size of the cell in micrometres (μm). (3 marks)
Mark Scheme:
1. Convert image size from mm to μm: 24 mm × 1000 = 24,000 μm (1 mark)
2. Use Actual size = Image size ÷ Magnification: 24,000 μm ÷ 400 (1 mark)
3. Actual size = 60 μm (1 mark)
